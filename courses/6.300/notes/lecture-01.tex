\subsection{Lecture: Pitch vs. Timbre}

Consider the following graphs of air pressure over time
detected by a microphone listening to different musical instruments.
\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{lecture-01/instrument-signals.png}}
\end{center}
Notice how each graph has the same period of $\frac{1}{262}$ seconds.
Consequently, each instrument's sound has the \emph{same pitch}:
262 Hz to be precise (or approximately C4).
However, each instrument's sound has a \emph{different timbre}.
This is because the ``shape'' of each graph
across a single period differs greatly.

Signal processing, spiritually, is the study of
looking beyond just the pitch of a signal.
How can we quantitatively describe
the ``shape'' of a signal across one of its periods?

\subsection{Lecture: Fourier Series}

Here's the driving mathematical fact behind Fourier series.

\begin{theorem}{Fourier Analysis}
    Any (sufficiently well-behaved) signal $f(t)$ with period $T$
    can be expressed as a sum of sinusoids.
    \[
    f(t) = \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty}
    (c_k \cos (k \omega_0 t) + d_k \sin (k \omega_0 t))
    \]
    In the above, $\omega_0 := \frac{2\pi}{T}$ is the \textbf{fundamental frequency},
    and the coefficients $\{c_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$ and $\{d_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$
    are the following.
    \[
    c_0 = \dfrac{1}{T} \int_0^T f(t) \, \mathrm{d}t ~~~ \parallel ~~~
    c_{\ell} = \dfrac{2}{T} \int_0^T f(t) \cos(\ell \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t ~~~ \parallel ~~~
    d_{\ell} = \dfrac{2}{T} \int_0^T f(t) \sin( \ell \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d} t.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Handwaving: assume that $f(t)$ can be expressed as a sum of sinusoids,
    then derive the formulas for $\{c_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$ and $\{d_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$.

    The $c_0$ coefficient is obvious.  For the others, recall for any $A, B \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ that:
    \[
    \int_0^T \cos(A \omega_0 t) \cos(B \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t =
    \int_0^T \dfrac{1}{2} \cos((A - B)\omega_0 t) + \dfrac{1}{2} \cos((A + B)\omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t =
    \begin{cases}
    T/2 & \text{ if } A = B. \\
    0 & \text{ otherwise. }
    \end{cases} \]
    \[
    \int_0^T \sin(A \omega_0 t) \cos(B \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t =
    \int_0^T \dfrac{1}{2} \sin((A - B) \omega_0 t) + \dfrac{1}{2} \sin((A + B) \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t = 0.
    \]
    \[
    \int_0^T \sin(A \omega_0 t) \sin(B \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t =
    \int_0^T \dfrac{1}{2} \cos((A - B) \omega_0 t) - \dfrac{1}{2} \cos((A + B) \omega_0 t) \, \mathrm{d}t = 
    \begin{cases}
    T/2 & \text{ if } A = B. \\
    0 & \text{ otherwise. }
    \end{cases}
    \]
    These imply the formulas.
    In some sense, there's a sort of orthogonal basis here or whatever. ~ $\blacksquare$
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    The relationship $\omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{T}$ must not be broken!
    For example, if a signal has fundamental frequency $\omega_0 = 10\pi$,
    then we must have $T = 0.2$, 
    so its Fourier coefficients must be computed
    by integrating over an interval of length $0.2$.
\end{remark}

The fundamental frequency of a signal captures its pitch,
but the coefficients $\{c_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$ and $\{d_k\}_{k = 0}^{\infty}$
capture its timbre.
So we might visualize the \textbf{harmonic structure} of a signal
by plotting $m_k := \sqrt{c_k^2 + d_k^2}$ against
the harmonic number $k$.

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{lecture-01/harmonic-structure.png}}
\end{center}

This tells us that the second harmonic of this signal
is about twice as strong as its first harmonic (or fundamental).

\begin{remark}
    The choice of plotting $m_k := \sqrt{c_k^2 + d_k^2}$
    comes from the following rewrite.
    \begin{align*}
        f(t) = \ & \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty} (c_k \cos (k \omega_0 t) + d_k \sin (k \omega_0 t)) \\
        = \ & \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty} m_k \cos(k \omega_0 t - \phi_k) \text{ where }
        m_k := \sqrt{c_k^2 + d_k^2} \text{ and } \phi_k := \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{d_k}{c_k}\right).
    \end{align*}
    So the measurement $m_k$ captures the strength of
    the part of the signal with frequency $k \omega_0$.
\end{remark}

Thus, there are two ways we can look at a signal:
\begin{itemize}
    \item As a function of time: $f(t)$.
    \item As a function of frequency: $k \mapsto (\omega_0, m_k, \phi_k)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recitation: Computing Fourier Series}

Recall that $\cos(t)$ is even and $\sin(t)$ is odd.  Therefore,
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $f(t)$ is even,
    then $f(t) \sin(k\omega_0 t)$ is odd for all $k$,
    so $d_k = 0$ for all $k$.
    \item If $f(t)$ is odd,
    then $f(t) \cos(k\omega_0 t)$ is odd for all $k$,
    so $c_k = 0$ for all $k$.
\end{itemize}
Thus, if a signal is known to be even or odd,
its Fourier series can be computed in half the usual number of steps.

\begin{problem}
    Determine the Fourier coefficients of $f(t) = |\sin(t)|$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    This is even with period $T = \pi$ and fundamental frequency $\omega_0 = 2$.
    Thus, $d_k = 0$ and:
    \[
    c_k = \dfrac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin(t) \cos(2kt) \, \mathrm{d}t
    = \dfrac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \sin((1 + 2k)t) + \sin((1 - 2k)t) \, \mathrm{d}t
    = \dfrac{1}{\pi} \left[ \dfrac{2}{1 + 2k} + \dfrac{2}{1 - 2k} \right]
    = \dfrac{-4}{\pi(4k^2 - 1)}. \]
    Separately, we compute $c_0 = \frac{2}{\pi}$ as well.
\end{solution}
