\subsection{Lecture: Wireless Communication (is Hard)}

\begin{problem}
    We have a signal that represents human speech,
    containing frequencies from 200 Hz to 3000 Hz.
    We would like to communicate this signal wirelessly
    by transmitting and receiving electromagnetic waves via antenna.

    How long must such an antenna be to transmit a 200–3000 Hz signal?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    According to \emph{physics},
    a 200 Hz signal would have the longest wavelength, that being:
    \[ \lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \ \text{m/s}}{200 \ \text{Hz}}
    = 1.5 \times 10^6 \ \text{m} = 1500 \ \text{km}. \]
    Also according to \emph{physics},
    the optimal length of an antenna is on the order of
    the wavelength of the signal it seeks to transmit.
    So we would need an antenna $1500$ kilometers long\dots
\end{solution}

\begin{remark}
    In an antenna, electrons oscillate back and forth along its length;
    to achieve resonance, the wavelength of the signal produced by an antenna
    must be on the order of the antenna's length.
\end{remark}

The issue, of course, is that most antennas are $10$ centimeters long,
not $1500$ kilometers.

\begin{problem}
    What frequencies would be produced by an antenna that is $10$ centimeters long?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    Again by \emph{physics},
    we would expect frequencies on the order of
    $ f = \frac{c}{\lambda} \sim \frac{3 \times 10^8 \ \text{m/s}}{10 \ \text{cm}}
    \approx 3 \ \text{GHz}$.
\end{solution}

The challenge is that most signals (not just speech!)
have frequencies that are not at all close to $3$ GHz.
We need to find some way to \textbf{modulate} these signals
so that their frequency fits within the desired range.

\subsection{Lecture: Amplitude Modulation}

To increase the frequencies present within a CT signal,
we might hope to just, say, shift its Fourier transform to the right, like so:

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/bad-modulation}}
\end{center}

Recall from
\href{6.html#lecture-and-recitation-properties-of-the-ct-fourier-transform}{Lecture 6}
that the effect of this is that $y(t) = e^{j \omega_c t} x(t)$.
This won't work, though, since the resulting signal $y(t)$ will then be complex-valued,
and thus not physically transmittable.
The trick is to try this instead:

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/good-modulation}}
\end{center}

The effect of the above is that $y(t) = 2\cos(\omega_c t) x(t)$,
which is real-valued.  You can even see below
how $y(t)$ has a very high frequency for large $\omega_c$,
yet still communicates the ``shape'' of $x(t)$.

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/modulation-graph}}
\end{center}

\textbf{Definition.}
The system $x(t) \mapsto \cos(\omega_c t) x(t)$ is called
\textbf{amplitude modulation}.

\begin{remark}
    Note that amplitude modulation is not an LTI system:
    it's not time-invariant.

    Additionally, note that we switched from $y(t) = 2\cos(\omega_c t) x(t)$
    to $y(t) = \cos(\omega_c t) x(t)$,
    dropping the factor of $2$.  The effect of this
    is that the amplitudes in $Y(\omega)$ will be half of those in $X(\omega)$.
    (We'll account for this later.)
\end{remark}

Here's another way to see why amplitude modulation
affects the frequency domain in the way it does.

\begin{theorem}{Filtering Dual}
    If $z(t) = x(t) \cdot y(t)$, then
    $Z(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi} (X * Y)(\omega)$.
    The same goes for DT signals.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    This is the dual of the statement that
    the Fourier transform of a convolution
    equals the product of the Fourier transforms.

    Here's the full computations written out for reference;
    there's nothing new here.
    \begin{align*}
        Z(\omega) & = \int_{t = -\infty}^{\infty} x(t) y(t) e^{-j \omega t} \, \mathrm{d}t \\
        & = \int_{t = -\infty}^{\infty} x(t) \left[
            \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\lambda = -\infty}^{\infty}
            Y(\lambda) e^{j \lambda t} \, \mathrm{d}\lambda
        \right] e^{-j \omega t} \, \mathrm{d}t \\
        & = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\lambda = -\infty}^{\infty} Y(\lambda) \left[
            \int_{t = -\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-j(\omega - \lambda)t} \, \mathrm{d}t
        \right] \, \mathrm{d}\lambda \\
        & = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{\lambda = -\infty}^{\infty}
        Y(\lambda) \left[ X(\omega - \lambda) \right] \, \mathrm{d}\lambda
        = \frac{1}{2\pi}(X * Y)(\omega).
    \end{align*}
    And the same goes for DT signals, expectedly. ~ $\blacksquare$
\end{proof}

And so the point is that
multiplying $x(t)$ by $c(t) := \cos(\omega_c t)$
is the same as convolving $X(\omega)$
with $C(\omega) := \pi \cdot \left(\delta(\omega_c) + \delta(-\omega_c)\right)$
and dividing the result by $2\pi$,
which has the effect shown below.

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/mod-convolution}}
\end{center}

\subsection{Lecture: Synchronous Demodulation}

After transmitting the modulated signal $y(t) = x(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$,
what happens on the receiving end—how can we demodulate $y(t)$
to retrieve the original signal $x(t)$?

\textbf{Definition.} The system $y(t) \mapsto \cos(\omega_c t) y(t)$
is called \textbf{synchronous demodulation}.

Yes, that is the exact same system as amplitude modulation.
Nonetheless, it works!

\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{lecture-11/demod-convolution}}
\end{center}

We can see the same effect working in the time domain, too:
\[ z(t) = \cos(\omega_c t) y(t) = \cos^2(\omega_c t) x(t)
= \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\omega_c t)\right) x(t). \]
The procedure, to be explicit,
is to first perform synchronous demodulation,
apply a low-pass filter,
and then multiply by $2$.

\subsection{Lecture: Amplitude Modulation with Carrier}

Importantly, the carrier signal $\cos(\omega_c t)$ used in amplitude modulation
must match the carrier signal $\cos(\omega_c t)$ used in synchronous demodulation.
What happens if the two carrier signal used differ by a phase shift $\phi$, though?
\[ z(t) = \cos(\omega_c t) \cos(\omega_c t + \phi) x(t)
= \frac{1}{2} x(t) \left(\cos(\phi) + \cos(2 \omega_c t + \phi)\right)
= \frac{1}{2} x(t) \cos(\phi) + \frac{1}{2}x(t) \cos(2 \omega_c t + \phi). \]
The latter of the two RHS terms will get filtered out via the LPF.
But the former of those two terms $\frac{1}{2}x(t) \cos(\phi)$
might experience \emph{fading} for unlucky values of $\phi$
(say, $\phi \approx \frac{\pi}{2}$).

Let's consider an entirely new approach to modulation and demodulation.
\begin{quote}
    \textbf{Approach (AM with Carrier).}
    Suppose we wish to transmit a signal $x(t)$
    using an antenna.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Pick a very high frequency $\omega_c$,
        along with a constant $C > |x(t)|$.
        \item Use the antenna to transmit the E/M wave
        $z(t) := \left(x(t) + C\right) \cos(\omega_c t)$.
        \item On the receiving end,
        apply a \emph{peak detector} to $z(t)$
        and subtract $C$ to retrieve the original signal $x(t)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{quote}
Notice how this approach doesn't even use synchronous demodulation;
the demodulation is the peak detector itself.
\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/carrier-transmission}}
\end{center}
The signal $z(t)$ that is transmitted has a special property:
its ``envelope'' traces out the shape of $x(t) + C$, as shown above.
Note that this property only holds if $C > |x(t)|$.
\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{lecture-11/peak-detector}}
\end{center}
On the receiving end, reading out that ``envelope'' is not very hard to do in hardware:
all it takes is an RC circuit with properly-tuned components.
Thus, the receiver just has to read the envelope $y(t)$,
noting that $x(t) \approx y(t) - C$.

The downside of this approach is that
it doesn't make use of synchronous demodulation;
the receiver can't distinguish between multiple transmissions added together,
even if each transmitter uses a different value for $\omega_c$.

Another downside of this approach
is the \emph{power} necessary to produce the E/M wave
$z(t) := x(t) \cos(\omega_c t) + C \cos(\omega_c t)$.

\begin{problem}
    Compare the power
    of the E/M wave $x(t) \cos(\omega_c t) + C \cos(\omega_c t)$
    to that of $x(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
    Let's say $x_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the peak value of $x(t)$,
    and $x_{\mathrm{rms}}$ is the root-mean-square value of $x(t)$.
    \begin{itemize} 
        \item According to \emph{physics}, the power carried by $x(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$
        is proportional to $x_{\mathrm{rms}}^2$.
        \item Note that we require $C > x_{\mathrm{p}}$.
        According to \emph{physics}, the power carried by $C \cos(\omega_c t)$
        is proportional to $C^2 > x_{\mathrm{p}}^2$.
    \end{itemize}
    The unfortunate fact, however, is that
    typically $x_{\mathrm{p}} > 35 \cdot x_{\mathrm{rms}}$.
    \begin{center}
        \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/speech}}
    \end{center}
    So the power needed to transmit the carrier
    tends to be more than $1225$ times
    the power needed to transmit the message itself.
    This is an acceptable tradeoff for 50 kW broadcast radio, perhaps,
    but not for, say, phone calls and cell phone batteries.
\end{solution}

There are, of course, technologies developed to fix some of these issues;
we won't get into those details.

\subsection{Recitation: Implementing Band-Pass Filters (BPFs)}

Recalling \href{10.html#recitation-filter-types}{Lecture 10},
we already have designed systems that function as low-pass filters.
Now let's design a band-pass filter instead:
a filter that accepts frequencies within some margin $\pm \Omega_d$
of either $\Omega_c$ or $-\Omega_c$.

\begin{remark}
    As a reminder, we are working with DT signals,
    so all frequencies $\Omega$ are to be interpreted modulo $2\pi$.
\end{remark}

Here's one strategy that's sure to work.

\begin{theorem}{BPF \#1}
    Let $h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n]$ denote the unit-sample response
    of a low-pass filter.  Then a system whose unit-sample response is
    $h[n] := h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n] \cdot \cos(\Omega_c n)$
    is a band-pass filter.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We would like the frequency response $H(\Omega)$ of our BPF system
    to look like two copies of that of an LPF system,
    one centered at $\Omega = -\Omega_c$,
    and another centered at $\Omega = \Omega_c$.

    To construct such a frequency response,
    let's just steal an idea from amplitude modulation.
    \begin{center}
        \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{lecture-11/BPF}}
    \end{center}
    It suffices to take the frequency response $H_{\mathrm{LPF}}(\Omega)$
    of the LPF filter and convolve it with
    $C(\Omega) := \pi \cdot \left(\delta[-\Omega_c] + \delta[\Omega_c]\right)$.

    But just as with amplitude modulation
    (see the ``Filtering Dual'' theorem from earlier),
    convolving with $C(\Omega)$ is the same as
    multiplying by $c[n] := \cos(\Omega_c n)$.
    So we want $h[n] = h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n] \cdot c[n]
    = h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n] \cdot \cos(\Omega_c n)$. ~ $\blacksquare$
\end{proof}

Instead of defining such a system by its unit-sample response, however,
let's try defining it in the time domain.

\begin{theorem}{BPF \#2}
    The BPF we seek is described by the cascading system depicted below.
    \begin{center}
        \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{lecture-11/BPF-cascade}}
    \end{center}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Recalling the relationship between a system and its unit-sample response from
    \href{9.html#lecture-and-recitation-unit-sample-response-and-convolution}{Lecture 9},
    we can directly read off:
    \begin{align*}
        y[n] & = (x * h)[n] \\
        & = \sum_{m = -\infty}^{\infty} x[m] \cdot h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n - m]
        \cos(\Omega_c(n - m)) \\
        & = \sum_{m = -\infty}^{\infty} x[m] \cdot h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n - m]
        \left(\cos(\Omega_c n) \cos(\Omega_c m) + \sin(\Omega_c n) \sin(\Omega_c m)\right) \\
        & = \underbrace{\left(
            \sum_{m = -\infty}^{\infty} x[m] \cos(\Omega_c m) \cdot
            h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n - m]
        \right)}_{
            x[n] \cos(\Omega_c n) \ * \ h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n]
        } \cdot \cos(\Omega_c n) + \underbrace{\left(
            \sum_{m = -\infty}^{\infty} x[m] \sin(\Omega_c m) \cdot
            h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n - m]
        \right)}_{
            x[n] \sin(\Omega_c n) \ * \ h_{\mathrm{LPF}}[n]
        } \cdot \sin(\Omega_c n)
    \end{align*}
    And the above is exactly what the cascading system above computes. ~ $\blacksquare$
\end{proof}

Philosophically, here's one way to think about
what the system above is actually accomplishing.

\begin{quote}
    % \textbf{Philosophy of BPF \#2.}
    % Recall from
    % \href{7.html#lecture-and-recitation-properties-of-the-dt-fourier-transform}{Lecture 7}
    % that the Fourier transform of a real DT signal $f[n]$
    % satisfies $F(-\Omega) = \overline{F(\Omega)}$.
    % Therefore, if we split $F(\Omega)$ into its real and imaginary components
    % $A(\Omega) + j B(\Omega)$, then $A(\Omega)$ is even and $B(\Omega)$ is odd.

    % When $X(\Omega)$ is even,
    % the ``top chain'', alone, of the BPF \#2 system
    % works perfectly as a band-pass filter.
    % \begin{center}
    %     \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.2\textwidth]{lecture-11/BPF-even}}
    % \end{center}
    % When $X(\Omega)$ is odd,
    % the ``bottom chain'', alone, of the BPF \#2 system
    % works perfectly as a band-pass filter.
    % \begin{center}
    %     \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.2\textwidth]{lecture-11/BPF-odd}}
    % \end{center}
    % And importantly, when $X(\Omega)$ is odd,
    % feeing $X(\Omega)$ into the ``top chain'' of the BPF \#2 system
    % will always return nothing,
    % and same for when an even $X(\Omega)$ is fed into the ``bottom chain''.
    % \begin{center}
    %     \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.2\textwidth]{lecture-11/BPF-cancel}}
    % \end{center}
    % Thus, BPF \#2 effectively runs $A(\Omega)$ and $j B(\Omega)$
    % through separate band-pass filters and sums the results.

    \textbf{Philosophy of BPF \#2.}
    \textcolor{red}{
        Give a convincing write-up of how BPF \#2 works.
        The idea, roughly speaking, is that the frequency response $X(\Omega)$
        has two relevant sections: one section over the domain
        $[-\Omega_c \pm \Omega_d]$, and another section over the domain
        $[+\Omega_c \pm \Omega_d]$.  The challenge is that
        there is no easy way to isolate one of the two sections on their own.
    }

    \textcolor{red}{
        However, by multiplying by $\cos(\Omega_c n)$,
        we can isolate their sum,
        and by multiplying by $\sin(\Omega_c n)$,
        we can isolate their difference.
        The sum and the difference, together, is sufficient data
        for isolating each section individually.
    }

    \textcolor{red}{
        Unfortunately, the 6.300 notes
        do not provide helpful diagrams for communicating this point.
    }
\end{quote}

\begin{remark}
    Note that multiplying by $s[n] := \sin(\Omega_c n)$
    is the same as convolving by
    $S(\Omega) := \frac{\pi}{j} \cdot \left(\delta[\Omega_c] - \delta[-\Omega_c]\right)$.

    Also recall from \href{2.html#recitation-symmetry-in-fourier-series}{Lecture 2}
    that every signal is the sum of an even signal and an odd signal.
    The fact that $f[n]$ is a real signal is actually not necessary;
    BPF \#2 works even when $f[n]$ is complex-valued (as it should!).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Recitation: DSB-AM vs. SSB-SC}

The $x(t) \cos(\omega_c t)$ transmission strategy has a more complicated name:
\emph{Double Sideband Amplitude Modulation (DSB-AM)}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item It's called \emph{Double Sideband} because, in the frequency domain,
    the transform $X(\omega)$ appears \emph{twice} in the output.
    \item It's called \emph{amplitude modulation} because, in the time domain,
    the signal $x(t)$ modulates the amplitude of $\cos(\omega_c t)$.
\end{itemize}
The downside of DSB-AM is in the \emph{Double Sideband} property.
There is an optimization to be made that can
cut the cost of transmitting messages by a factor of two.
It's called \emph{Single Sideband Suppressed Carrier (SSB-SC)},
and it looks like this:
\begin{center}
    \frame{\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{lecture-11/SSB-SC}}
\end{center}
In the above, $\omega_m$ is a frequency such that
all of the nonzero behavior of $X(\omega)$ lives within the interval
$[-\omega_m, \omega_m]$.
